The Chemistry of Batteries

Atoms' electronegativity attracts electrons, causing a pull force that can be transferred to powering devices.

1 / 11

Who pulls harder on electrons?

Electronegativity is an atom's pull on electrons. Copper pulls harder, so zinc's electrons move over. Zinc is oxidized and copper is reduced: a redox reaction.

2 / 11

Copper ions steal zinc's electrons

In copper sulfate, Cu²⁺ ions that touch the zinc take two electrons each and become solid copper, coating the strip. The zinc dissolves as Zn²⁺.

Why heat? Electrons sit at lower energy on copper than on zinc. When they hop straight across, the energy they give up goes into jostling nearby atoms and water molecules, which we feel as heat.

3 / 11

Split the reaction in two

Put each metal in its own beaker. Now zinc's electrons can't reach the copper ions, and nothing happens.

4 / 11

Oxidation at the anode

Zinc atoms lose two electrons and dissolve as Zn²⁺. This electrode is the anode (−).

OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

5 / 11

Reduction at the cathode

Cu²⁺ ions gain two electrons and plate out as copper. This electrode is the cathode (+).

AN OX, RED CAT

6 / 11

Add a wire: a spark, then nothing

Electrons rush through the wire, then stop. Charge builds up in each beaker and pushes back.

7 / 11

The salt bridge completes the circuit

NO₃⁻ and K⁺ ions drift through the bridge and cancel the charge build-up. Electrons now flow steadily. It's a battery.

Electrons never cross the solutions. Only ions do.

8 / 11

Where 1.10 volts comes from

Voltage is the gap between how strongly each side pulls electrons:

E°cathode − E°anode = 1.10 V
9 / 11

The battery in your phone

Your phone runs on a lithium-ion battery. Electrons leave its negative terminal, flow through the phone's circuits to power it, and return to the positive terminal.

10 / 11

Zoom in: inside a lithium-ion cell

Electrons take the outside path through the phone. At the same time, Li⁺ ions cross the separator inside, from the graphite anode to the metal-oxide cathode. The separator does the salt bridge's job.

11 / 11

Plugging in runs it backward

The charger pushes electrons back to the anode, and Li⁺ ions follow, tucking back between the graphite layers. Now the battery can power your phone again.
